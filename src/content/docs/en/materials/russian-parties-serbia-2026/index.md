Serbia's political parties should be distinguished as **(1) organisations legally registered as Russian national-minority parties**, **(2) Serbian parties that advocate closer relations with Russia**, and **(3) allegations of direct ties to Russian state institutions**. Similar policies alone do not prove financial or organisational dependence.

A registered legal representative need not be party president. Minority classification does not establish support from the ethnic Russian community.[^1]

## What parties actually propose

**Russian Party (2013):** its original programme advocates a Eurasian Union, opposition to NATO, Russian-language education, a maximum of 150 MPs, 10% of the budget for farming, a tax on certain unmarried employees, and restrictions on abortion with a medical exception. This platform must not be attributed to other Russian-minority parties.[^2]

**Russian–Serbian Unity (2026):** according to Euronews Serbia, its proposals include ending EU accession talks, involving Russia and China in Kosovo negotiations, joining BRICS, banning foreign-funded NGOs and increasing trade with Russia. These are stated proposals, not implemented measures.[^3]

## Pro-Russian positions without Russian-minority status

- [Serbian People's Party](/en/parties/srpska-narodna-partija/) (SNP): names Nenad Popović as founder and president and emphasises his parliamentary and economic links with Russia.[^4]
- [Oathkeepers](/en/parties/srpska-stranka-zavetnici/): advocate closer ties with Russia and oppose sanctions on Moscow, according to RFE/RL's 2024 report.[^5]
- [Movement of Socialists](/en/parties/pokret-socijalista/): in 2025 Aleksandar Vulin publicly supported BRICS and allied relations with Russia and China.[^6]

These parties are not registered under the Russian-minority classification. Pro-Russian positions do not alone prove links to Russian state authorities.

## Representation and electoral practice

In the final 25 October 2026 election results, the **Russian Party** received **6,503 votes and no seats**, while **Russian–Serbian Unity** received **4,286 votes and no seats**. BUNT — Real Serbia appeared on a joint list with the People's Party rather than as a separate minority electoral list.[^7]

RFE/RL and BIRN reported concerns from analysts about the possible electoral use of minority-party status. These are **attributed allegations and interpretations**, not judicial findings or proof about the ethnic identities of party members.[^8]
